**Where:** outdoors, venue posted by the Friday before. Opens with the
[ten-minute detox](/lectures/01-not-that-serious/).

Ask people where they last solved something and a suspicious number say the
shower. This lecture is about whether that is a real effect or a story we
enjoy telling, and the answer turns out to be: partly real, and more specific
than the story.

## Not all breaks are the same break

Baird and colleagues (2012, *Psychological Science*) gave people a creativity
task, then an incubation period, then the task again. Four different breaks:

| What people did during the break | Improvement on problems they'd already seen |
| --- | --- |
| An undemanding task (easy, automatic, attention to spare) | Yes — substantially |
| A demanding task | No |
| Rest, with nothing to do | No |
| No break at all | No |

The undemanding condition was also the one where people reported the most
mind-wandering — and, notably, *not* the one where they reported deliberately
thinking about the problem. The improvement showed up only on problems they
had already encountered, which is what makes this an incubation effect rather
than a general creativity boost.

Two things fall out of that table. The first is that "take a break" is bad
advice; the finding is narrower. Doing nothing at all did not help. Doing
something hard did not help. The thing that helped was an easy task that
occupied your hands and left your attention loose — which is a fairly exact
description of washing dishes, walking a familiar route, or standing in a
shower.

The second is that this is one paradigm with one task, and the mind-wandering
literature has a large asterisk on it: only constructive mind-wandering looks
like this. Ruminative mind-wandering — the week 5 material — tracks with
anxiety and low mood, not with creative incubation. The same idle mind does
both. Nothing about an empty ten minutes guarantees you the good one.

## Why finishing matters

Sophie Leroy (2009) named a related effect *attention residue*: when you
switch away from a task before finishing it, part of your attention stays
stuck on the one you left, and your performance on the next thing suffers.
Residue is worse when the first task was left unresolved.

Put that next to the incubation finding and the picture gets less comfortable.
The undemanding-task effect needs your attention to be free. Attention residue
says it usually isn't, because most of us are switching between things we
haven't finished. The empty slot only works if it is actually empty.

## The part that is my argument, not a finding

Here is a hypothesis the course is built around, offered as a hypothesis:

The slots where this incubation used to happen were never scheduled. They were
the walk, the commute, the cooking, the queue, the shower, the ten minutes
before sleep. None of them were designed as thinking time. They were simply
the leftovers — undemanding tasks with attention to spare, exactly the
condition Baird found to work.

And most of them now have something in them. The cooking has a podcast. The
walk has a playlist. The queue has a phone. The meal has a second screen, or
was ordered rather than made. Each substitution is individually reasonable and
individually pleasant, and together they may have quietly deleted the only
part of the day that was doing the incubating.

I want to be clear about the status of that paragraph. Baird 2012 is a
finding. Leroy 2009 is a finding. "Modern life has eliminated the incubation
slot and that is why people feel stuck in loops" is an inference across those
findings that nobody has tested directly, and it has an obvious competing
explanation: people who feel stuck may seek out distraction rather than the
other way round. Do not repeat it as science. Do treat it as the most
interesting thing in this lecture, and go and check it on yourself.

## Discussion

- Name a slot in your week that was empty five years ago and isn't now. What
  went into it, and would you actually give it back?
- The shower is the last common slot that resists audio for purely logistical
  reasons. Is that why it keeps showing up in the anecdotes?
- If the mechanism is an undemanding task rather than rest, then "do nothing"
  is the wrong prescription and "do the dishes" is the right one. Does that
  match your own experience, or contradict it?
