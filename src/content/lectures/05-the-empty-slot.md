**Where:** outdoors, venue posted by the Friday before. Opens with the
[ten-minute detox](/lectures/01-not-that-serious/).

Week 2 described the spiral: repetitive, self-focused, feels like work,
resolves nothing. It did not explain the most annoying thing about it, which
is the *repetition*. Why does the same thought come back at 2am having made no
progress since 11pm?

This lecture proposes an answer, and it is the claim the course is named
after: **a loop repeats because it never gets the conditions under which
thinking finishes.** Not because you are bad at thinking, and not because the
problem is unusually hard. Because the slot where it would have completed is
occupied.

Unfinished thinking. That is the whole title, and this is the week it stops
being a phrase on a handbook page and starts being an argument you can test.
Four weeks of catching yourself in the act came first on purpose — the
mechanism means very little until you have a stack of your own examples to
hold it against.

To make that argument we need to know what those conditions actually are,
which is where the research comes in. Ask people where they last solved
something and a suspicious number say the shower.

## Not all breaks are the same break

Baird and colleagues (2012, *Psychological Science*) gave people a creativity
task, then an incubation period, then the task again. Four different breaks:

| What people did during the break | Improvement on problems they'd already seen |
| --- | --- |
| An undemanding task (easy, automatic, attention to spare) | Yes — substantially |
| A demanding task | No |
| Rest, with nothing to do | No |
| No break at all | No |

The undemanding condition was also the one where people reported the most
mind-wandering — and, notably, *not* the one where they reported deliberately
thinking about the problem. The improvement showed up only on problems they
had already encountered, which is what makes this an incubation effect rather
than a general creativity boost.

Two things fall out of that table. The first is that "take a break" is bad
advice; the finding is narrower. Doing nothing at all did not help. Doing
something hard did not help. The thing that helped was an easy task that
occupied your hands and left your attention loose — which is a fairly exact
description of washing dishes, walking a familiar route, or standing in a
shower.

The second is that this is one paradigm with one task, and the mind-wandering
literature has a large asterisk on it: only constructive mind-wandering looks
like this. Ruminative mind-wandering — the week 2 material — tracks with
anxiety and low mood, not with creative incubation. The same idle mind does
both. Nothing about an empty ten minutes guarantees you the good one.

## Why finishing matters

Sophie Leroy (2009) named a related effect *attention residue*: when you
switch away from a task before finishing it, part of your attention stays
stuck on the one you left, and your performance on the next thing suffers.
Residue is worse when the first task was left unresolved.

Put that next to the incubation finding and the picture gets less comfortable.
The undemanding-task effect needs your attention to be free. Attention residue
says it usually isn't, because most of us are switching between things we
haven't finished. The empty slot only works if it is actually empty.

Residue also gives us the mechanism for the repetition. An unresolved thing
you walked away from is exactly the case where residue is worst — so it comes
back. Not because it is important. Because it is unfinished, and unfinished
things keep a claim on your attention.

## Back to the spiral

Now put the whole thing together, because this is the claim the course rests
on.

A spiral is an unfinished piece of thinking. Unfinished things generate
residue, so they return. They would finish, on the incubation account, given
an undemanding task with attention to spare — the walk, the dishes, the
shower. Deny them that and they do not resolve; they just keep coming back to
the front of the queue, and each return feels like new thinking while being
the same lap.

Which reframes the whole course. If this is right, the problem was never that
you think too much. It is that the thinking never gets to *complete*, so it
re-runs — and every strategy that keeps your attention fully occupied is
therefore not a break from the spiral but a way of preserving it.

That is also why the two crits that look least like this course belong in
it. [The sandwich](/sessions/05-the-sandwich/) is an undemanding task with
nothing else running — the incubation condition, built on purpose. The
[ten minutes](/sessions/03-ten-minutes-guided/) are the same slot, protected
by being scheduled. Neither is a wellness gesture; both are attempts to
manufacture the condition under which a loop can close.

## The part that is my argument, not a finding

Here is the version of that claim I actually suspect is true, offered as a
suspicion:

The slots where this used to happen were never scheduled. They were the walk,
the commute, the cooking, the queue, the shower, the ten minutes before sleep.
None of them were designed as thinking time. They were simply the leftovers —
undemanding tasks with attention to spare, exactly the condition Baird found
to work.

And most of them now have something in them. The cooking has a podcast. The
walk has a playlist. The queue has a phone. The meal has a second screen, or
was ordered rather than made. Each substitution is individually reasonable and
individually pleasant, and together they may have quietly deleted the only
part of the day that was closing your open loops.

I want to be clear about the status of that. Baird 2012 is a finding. Leroy
2009 is a finding. "A spiral repeats because the incubation slot is occupied"
is an inference across those two that nobody has tested directly, and it has
an obvious competing explanation: people who feel stuck may seek out
distraction rather than the other way round. Do not repeat it as science. Do
treat it as the most interesting thing in this lecture — it is the course's
own best guess, and next week we go after the single biggest thing now sitting
in that slot.

## Discussion

- Name a slot in your week that was empty five years ago and isn't now. What
  went into it, and would you actually give it back?
- The shower is the last common slot that resists audio for purely logistical
  reasons. Is that why it keeps showing up in the anecdotes?
- If the mechanism is an undemanding task rather than rest, then "do nothing"
  is the wrong prescription and "do the dishes" is the right one. Does that
  match your own experience, or contradict it?
- Test the claim against a spiral of your own that actually did close. What
  closed it — an empty slot, or something else entirely? A counter-example is
  more useful to this lecture than a confirmation.
