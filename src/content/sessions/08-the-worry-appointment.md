This is the first of the crits the cohort chose. It came out of the vote on
[the anonymous thread](/assessments/the-anonymous-thread/), where some version
of "I make myself deal with it later" turned up more often than anything else.
What follows is the plan as it stands. If the vote reshuffles the back half
again, this is the kind of thing that moves.

## Before the session

Book yourself a worry appointment for this week. Fifteen minutes, a specific
time, somewhere you can sit. Put it in whatever you actually look at.

Then, between now and the crit, try the move. When you notice a worry
starting, write down one line about it and tell yourself you will do it
properly at the appointment. Go back to whatever you were doing. At the
appointment, open the list.

Nothing about this has to work. Turning up having failed at it is a result,
and a more interesting one than turning up having succeeded.

## In the session

Round one, the log. What did you postpone, how many times did the same thing
come back before the slot, and what actually happened when you sat down with
it. The most common report is that some of the list had evaporated and the
rest took four minutes. If that is not what happened to you, say so, because
that is the finding the room needs.

You can pass on this round without giving a reason, and you can describe a
worry in the vaguest terms you like. "One about money" is a complete entry.

Round two, the mechanism. Worry postponement asks you to believe two things.
That the loop will still be there later, and that later you will be able to
get through it. Which of those did your week actually support? A loop that
dissolves by the appointment was arguably never going to resolve. A loop that
is still burning at the appointment is a different animal, and worth naming
as one.

Round three, in pairs. Design the version you would actually keep doing. When,
where, how long, and what you do with the list at the end.

## Where the evidence sits

Borkovec, Wilkinson, Folensbee and Lerman (1983, *Behaviour Research and
Therapy*) borrowed this from insomnia treatment, where you get out of bed
rather than lie there failing to sleep. Their reasoning was that worry happens
anywhere because it has been paired with everywhere, so confining it to one
time and place might weaken the pairing. Two experiments with self-identified
worriers showed real reductions in daily worry against no treatment.

One honest wrinkle. Their instructions also told people to problem-solve and
to return attention to the present, so the effect cannot be pinned on the
postponing alone. That is worth knowing before you credit the appointment for
everything.

Dippel, Brosschot and Verkuil (2024, *International Journal of Cognitive
Therapy*) pooled seven randomised trials and 999 people. Worry postponement
beat simply recording your worries, with **d = 0.313** for how long people
worried and **d = 0.189** for how often. Both small. The authors also note
there is no long-term follow-up, so nobody knows whether this holds past a
month.

So it works, a bit, for a few weeks, and nobody has checked what happens after
that. This course would rather tell you that than sell you a habit.

## Afterwards

Log it, including if it did nothing. A technique that failed on you is more
useful to the room than another person confirming it works.
