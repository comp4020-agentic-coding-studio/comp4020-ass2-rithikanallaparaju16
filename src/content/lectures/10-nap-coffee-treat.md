Nothing is due in this course for the rest of semester. That is deliberate and
it is explained on the [Assessment](/assessments/) page. What it means for
today is that we can spend a whole lecture on the least glamorous question in
the course: when you are fried and spiralling in week 10, what actually helps?

The folk answer is a nap, a coffee, or something sweet. One of those is well
supported, one is genuinely double-edged in a way that matters specifically in
a course about rumination, and one appears to do the opposite of what everyone
believes.

| The fix | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| A nap | Well supported, with a strict duration rule |
| A coffee | Real but oversold, and it carries an anxiety risk worth knowing |
| Something sweet | The best evidence says it makes you *more* tired, not less |

## The nap, which works

Rosekind and colleagues (1994) ran the study everyone half-remembers: pilots
on real long-haul transpacific flights, given a 40-minute rest opportunity in
the cockpit. They slept about 26 minutes on average and showed clearly better
vigilance and physiological alertness than the no-nap group — with the benefit
still there through descent and landing, which is the part of a flight where
you would like your pilot alert.

A pedantic note in keeping with this course: the famous "34% better
performance, 54% better alertness" numbers attached to that study come from
the team's 1995 write-up in the *Journal of Sleep Research*, not the original
NASA memorandum everybody links to. Small thing. We care about it here.

Now the part that actually changes your behaviour. Brooks and Lack (2006,
*Sleep*) tested naps of 5, 10, 20 and 30 minutes in sleep-restricted people:

| Nap length | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 5 minutes | Almost nothing |
| **10 minutes** | Best overall — benefits came on immediately and lasted up to about 155 minutes |
| 20 minutes | Worked, but took about 35 minutes to show up |
| 30 minutes | Produced a period of **impaired** alertness first — sleep inertia |

So the advice is not "have a nap." It is "have a ten-minute nap," and the
half-hour version can leave you worse off before it leaves you better.

One important distinction the internet blurs constantly. Mednick, Nakayama and
Stickgold (2003, *Nature Neuroscience*) showed a nap can consolidate learning
about as well as a night's sleep — but that needs roughly 60 to 90 minutes,
and it is a *memory* effect, not an alertness one. Two literatures, opposite
advice. Ten to twenty minutes if you need to be sharp this afternoon; an hour
and a half if you have just learned something and want to keep it, and are
willing to feel terrible for the first twenty minutes after waking.

Also worth saying plainly: nobody has studied naps and rumination. That
connection — sleep on the loop and it might close — is this course's guess,
not a finding.

## The coffee, which is oversold

Caffeine blocks adenosine and makes you feel more alert. That much is not in
question. What is in question is whether it makes you *better than baseline*
or merely returns you to it.

Peter Rogers and colleagues (2010, *Neuropsychopharmacology*) tested 379
people after 16 hours without caffeine. Habitual consumers given caffeine
came back to baseline alertness — no net gain. Non-consumers did show a rise,
which was offset by increased anxiety and jitteriness. Rogers followed this up
in 2014 with a paper defending the position outright: caffeinated regular
consumers never actually exceed the alertness of people who never took it up.
This is contested rather than settled, but it is a serious argument, and it
reframes your morning coffee as maintenance rather than enhancement.

Then the bit that matters in *this* course. Caffeine is anxiogenic at dose. In
studies giving 400–750 mg to people with panic disorder, around half had a
panic attack, against none on placebo. That is a large dose and a clinical
population — and the honest counterweight arrived recently: Hoppe and
colleagues (2025, *Journal of Psychopharmacology*) gave a realistic 150 mg to
panic-disorder patients and controls in a double-blind crossover and found
**no** increase in subjective anxiety, contrary to their own hypothesis.

So: at everyday doses, probably fine for most people. At high doses, a real
risk. The European Food Safety Authority puts habitual safety at up to 400 mg
a day and 200 mg in a single dose, which is roughly two to four cups depending
on who made it. And if you are someone whose spirals come with a racing heart,
you already know more about this than the literature does.

The **coffee nap** is real, incidentally. Reyner and Horne (1997,
*Psychophysiology*) gave drivers 200 mg of caffeine plus a nap under 15
minutes and cut driving incidents to 9% of placebo, against 34% for caffeine
alone. Replication has been mixed — at least one later study found the
combination no better than either on its own — so file it under promising
rather than proven.

## The sweet treat, which does the opposite

This is where the folk theory falls over, and it falls over hard.

Mantantzis, Schlaghecken, Sünram-Lea and Maylor (2019, *Neuroscience &
Biobehavioral Reviews*) meta-analysed 176 effect sizes from 31 studies and
1,259 participants, asking whether carbohydrate improves mood. The title is
"Sugar rush or sugar crash?" and the answer is neither-then-yes:

- **No positive effect on any aspect of mood, at any time point.** Not on
  alertness, not on mood, not at 30 minutes, not at an hour.
- **Alertness was lower** within 60 minutes of consumption.
- **Fatigue was higher** within 30 minutes.

The sugar rush does not exist. What exists is a measurable dip, arriving right
about when you were counting on the lift.

Before anyone gets smug: this is a claim about *sugar*, not about treats. If a
biscuit and ten minutes away from your desk helps you, the plausible
mechanism is the ten minutes, the ritual, and the deliberate stop — not the
biscuit. That psychological route is not disproven; it is unstudied, which is
a different thing, and it is precisely the sort of confusion this course
exists to catch. You had a break and credited the sugar.

## What to reach for instead

Two things with better support than any of the above.

**Move.** Ensari and colleagues (2015, *Depression and Anxiety*) meta-analysed
single bouts of exercise and found reductions in state anxiety of around a
quarter of a standard deviation. Modest, not miraculous, and more than sugar
manages.

**Take a break that is actually a break.** Which brings the whole course back
around: [week 5](/lectures/05-the-empty-slot/) found that only *undemanding*
activity produces incubation, and [week 6](/lectures/06-the-blank-that-isnt/)
found a phone break measuring like no break at all. A walk works. A scroll
does not. The ten-minute nap is arguably the purest empty slot available to
you.

One negative result, for honesty's sake: a well-known 2011 study claiming
brief mental breaks restore focus failed to replicate in 2017. It is quoted
constantly. We are not quoting it.
