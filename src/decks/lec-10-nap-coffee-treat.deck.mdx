# The nap, the coffee and the sweet treat

Week 10. Nothing is due. So, the least glamorous question in the course.

---

## Commit first

Rank these three by how well the evidence supports them.

1. a nap
2. a coffee
3. something sweet

Write your order down. One of them does the opposite of what almost everyone
believes.

---

## The nap, which works

Rosekind and colleagues (1994) studied pilots on real long-haul transpacific
flights, given a 40-minute rest opportunity in the cockpit.

They slept about 26 minutes on average, and showed clearly better vigilance
and physiological alertness than the no-nap group. Still there through descent
and landing.

---

## A pedantic note, in keeping with this course

The famous "34% better performance, 54% better alertness" numbers come from
the team's 1995 write-up in the *Journal of Sleep Research*.

Not the original NASA memorandum everybody links to.

Small thing. We care about it here.

---

## The part that changes your behaviour

Brooks and Lack (2006, *Sleep*) tested naps of 5, 10, 20 and 30 minutes in
sleep-restricted people.

| Nap length | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 5 minutes | Almost nothing |
| **10 minutes** | Best overall. Benefits immediate, lasted about 155 minutes |
| 20 minutes | Worked, but took about 35 minutes to show up |
| 30 minutes | Produced **impaired** alertness first. Sleep inertia |

## Not "have a nap". Have a ten-minute nap

The half-hour version can leave you worse off before it leaves you better.

---

## Two literatures, and one gap

Mednick, Nakayama and Stickgold (2003, *Nature Neuroscience*) showed a nap can
consolidate learning about as well as a night's sleep. But that needs roughly
60 to 90 minutes, and it is a *memory* effect, not an alertness one.

**Nobody has studied naps and rumination.** Sleep on the loop and it might
close is this course's guess, not a finding.

---

## The coffee, which is oversold

Peter Rogers and colleagues (2010, *Neuropsychopharmacology*) tested 379
people after 16 hours without caffeine.

- habitual consumers given caffeine came back to **baseline**, with no net gain
- non-consumers did rise, and that was offset by increased anxiety and
  jitteriness

Rogers defended the position again in 2014. **Contested rather than settled**,
but it reframes your morning coffee as maintenance rather than enhancement.

---

## The anxiety risk, both sides

In studies giving **400–750 mg** to people with panic disorder, around half
had a panic attack, against none on placebo. That is a large dose and a
clinical population.

The honest counterweight. Hoppe and colleagues (2025, *Journal of
Psychopharmacology*) gave a realistic **150 mg** to panic-disorder patients and
controls in a double-blind crossover, and found **no** increase in subjective
anxiety. Contrary to their own hypothesis.

---

## The sweet treat

Mantantzis, Schlaghecken, Sünram-Lea and Maylor (2019, *Neuroscience &amp;
Biobehavioral Reviews*) meta-analysed 176 effect sizes from 31 studies and
1,259 participants.

- **no positive effect on any aspect of mood, at any time point**
- alertness **lower** within 60 minutes
- fatigue **higher** within 30 minutes

## The sugar rush does not exist

What exists is a measurable dip, arriving right about when you were counting
on the lift.

---

## Before anyone gets smug

This is a claim about *sugar*, not about treats.

If a biscuit and ten minutes away from your desk helps you, the plausible
mechanism is the ten minutes, the ritual and the deliberate stop.

That route is **unstudied, which is not the same as disproven**. You had a
break and credited the sugar.

---

## What to reach for instead

**Move.** Ensari and colleagues (2015, *Depression and Anxiety*) found single
bouts of exercise reduce state anxiety by around a quarter of a standard
deviation. Modest, not miraculous, and more than sugar manages.

**Take a break that is actually a break.** Week 5 found only *undemanding*
activity produces incubation. Week 6 found a phone break measuring like no
break at all.
