**Where.** Outdoors, venue posted by the Friday before. Opens with the
[ten-minute detox](/lectures/01-not-that-serious/).

Three weeks in, this course has handed you a sitting practice from South Asia,
a defusion technique from an American psychotherapy tradition, and an argument
for holding class outdoors built on a Californian neuroimaging study. None of
that is a problem. Not knowing it would be.

This lecture is about provenance. Where the techniques came from, what got
lost on the way here, and how to tell a real tradition from something a
blogger made up in 2014. The last one is not a digression. It is the same
skill the rest of the course teaches, applied to the course itself.

## What the ten minutes actually is

The standard story is that Jon Kabat-Zinn took Theravāda Buddhist vipassanā
practice, stripped the religion out, called it Mindfulness-Based Stress
Reduction, and handed it to medicine. Kabat-Zinn has written about the origins
himself (2011, *Contemporary Buddhism*), and he does not hide the lineage.

But the tidy version is wrong in an interesting way. Husgafvel (2019, also in
*Contemporary Buddhism*) went through the history and argues the
"MBSR equals vipassanā" account is far too simple. Kabat-Zinn was shaped
substantially by Mahāyāna and Zen teachers and by Tibetan Dzogchen, and MBSR's
philosophical footing diverges from Theravāda in ways that matter.

So the accurate sentence is the less quotable one. **Adapted from Buddhist
contemplative traditions, plural, and then renamed.** Which is worth saying
out loud, because "we do a secular ten minutes" quietly implies the ten
minutes was always secular, and it wasn't. Something was removed to get it
here. An ethical framework, a community, a teacher, a purpose beyond feeling
better. Whether that removal was a reasonable trade or a significant loss is a
genuinely open question you are allowed to have a view on.

## The pattern to watch for

Now the failure mode, because there is a repeatable one and once you see it
you will see it constantly.

**Ikigai.** You have met it as a four-circle Venn diagram. What you love,
what you're good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for. It is
presented as ancient Japanese wisdom. It is not. The diagram was made by a
British blogger, Marc Winn, in 2014, by taking a 2011 Spanish "purpose"
diagram by Andrés Zuzunaga and swapping one word for *ikigai*. The Japanese
concept is real and much older. Mieko Kamiya's *On the Meaning of Life*
(1966) is a serious treatment, and the concept has essentially nothing to do
with being paid. The neuroscientist Ken Mogi, who writes on the concept, says
plainly that the popular version is wrong.

**Hoʻoponopono.** Widely sold as a four-phrase mantra. *I'm sorry, please
forgive me, thank you, I love you.* That version was popularised in the 2000s
by Hew Len and Joe Vitale, propelled by an unverifiable story about a
psychiatric ward. Traditional hoʻoponopono is a communal family process for
repairing relationships, led by a *kahuna lapaʻau*. Morrnah Simeona's
adaptation in the 1980s is the legitimate step in between.

Look at what those two have in common.

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | A practice exists inside a community, with a teacher and obligations |
| 2 | A Western populariser compresses it into a graphic or a mantra |
| 3 | It recirculates as ancient authenticity, stripped of the community |
| 4 | The locus moves from *repairing something between people* to *optimising an individual* |

Step 4 is the one to notice. In both cases the practice was originally about
your relations with other people, and the export turned it into a private
self-improvement tool. That should be uncomfortable in a course that has so
far treated overthinking as a thing happening inside one head.

## A framework that disagrees with this course

Which brings us to the most useful challenge on the syllabus.

Gee, Dudgeon, Schultz, Hart and Kelly (2014), writing in *Working Together*,
set out an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander social and emotional
wellbeing framework built on **seven domains of connection**. To body; to mind
and emotions; to family and kinship; to community; to culture; to Country; and
to spirituality and ancestors. Mind and emotions is *one* of seven.

Read that against this course's structure and you will notice something
awkward. Almost everything here sits in that single domain. Catch your loop,
notice your layer, build your empty slot. The other six get treated as context
at best. A framework in which wellbeing is constituted by connection to
family, community, culture and Country is not offering a nicer vocabulary for
the same idea. It is making a different claim about where a mind is located.

I am not going to resolve that today, and I am not going to pretend the
course's individual framing is neutral. It is a choice, it is the dominant one
in the literature this course draws on, and it may be part of why the
techniques help less than everyone hopes.

## Where the rest came from

For the record, since we are keeping one.

- **The defusion stem**, "I'm having the thought that…", is from Steven
  Hayes' acceptance and commitment therapy, American, and about forty years
  old.
- **Going outside** rests on Bratman and colleagues (2015), Stanford. The
  related Japanese practice, *shinrin-yoku*, has a precise and unromantic
  origin. The term was coined in 1982 by Tomohide Akiyama, then head of
  Japan's Forestry Agency. Its research base is real but weaker than
  Bratman's. One review found 28 studies with methodological problems, and a
  recent meta-analysis rated every outcome "very low" certainty.
- **Slow breathing**, which someone always asks about, is the weakest of the
  lot. The famous six-breaths-a-minute figure rests on a narrative review
  rather than pooled trials; a pranayama meta-analysis found a small effect
  against *passive* control only, vanishing once high-risk-of-bias studies were
  excluded. And nobody has studied it on rumination. Adverse events cluster
  around the fast techniques, not the slow ones.
- **[Next week's material](/lectures/08-act-anyway/)**, Morita therapy, is
  Japanese, from around 1920. It is the one tradition on this list whose core
  idea contradicts most of what you have been taught about managing feelings.

## Discussion

- The four-circle ikigai is more useful to more people than the real concept
  ever was. Does that matter? Argue both sides.
- If a practice's community was part of the mechanism, then exporting the
  technique without it isn't simplification. It is removing an active
  ingredient. How would you test that?
- Take the seven-domain framework seriously for ten minutes. Which crit in
  this course would you redesign, and how?
- This course opens every class with a borrowed practice and has now spent a
  lecture on the ethics of borrowing. Is that sufficient, or is it just
  well-documented borrowing?
