## Why the dates look like that

A course about not adding to your own mental load should not add to your
mental load. So the assessment calendar is built around the rest of your
degree rather than around this course's own convenience.

Most courses put their heaviest assessment at the end, which means weeks 10
to 13 are where every deadline you have arrives at once, immediately before
exams. This course deliberately gets out of the way of that:

- **The forum post lands in the mid-semester break** — the one week with no
  classes and, usually, no other deadlines. It is a two-hour job placed where
  a two-hour job fits.
- **The capstone is due in week 9**, before anyone else's crunch starts.
- **The last three teaching weeks carry no deadline at all.** Those are the
  weeks you will be under most pressure from everything else, so the teaching
  turns to [what actually helps under load](/lectures/10-nap-coffee-treat/).
- **The log is due 24 hours after the last exam in the cohort** — not after
  yours, after everybody's. And it is a log, kept as you go, so there is
  nothing to write at the end beyond tidying the file. Nobody should ever be
  doing this course's work while revising for something else.

Put plainly: this course would rather be useful in week 11 than assessed in
week 11.

## Being honest about the evidence for that

The rest of this site holds itself to naming a real study for every claim, so
this page has to do the same, and here the answer is thinner than we would
like.

There is good institutional practice behind avoiding "assignment bunching" —
several universities publish programme-level assessment calendars for exactly
this reason — and there is a plausible mechanism, since student effort
concentrates sharply just before a deadline, so clustered deadlines stack
those spikes on top of each other. There is also decent evidence that
*flexibility* helps: a study across four terms and two universities found
that giving students extension coupons improved both mental health and
engagement.

What we could not find is a study isolating clustered against spread deadlines
and measuring wellbeing properly. So this calendar is **design reasoning, not
a finding.** It is the course practising what it preaches rather than a result
we can point at, and if you think it is wrong, the capstone is a reasonable
place to argue so.

One more thing about AI, since it comes up here first: you may use it for
grammar and presentation and to research techniques, and not to write any of
this. [Policies](/policies/) has the detail and the reasoning.
