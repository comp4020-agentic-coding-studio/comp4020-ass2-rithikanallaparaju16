Third of the room's picks. "Write it down" was the single most common
suggestion in the thread, which is exactly why it is getting the full
treatment rather than a nod. Still provisional, like the rest of the back
half.

## Before the session

Bring something to write with and on. Paper if you can. Nothing else.

## In the session

Fifteen minutes, writing, no stopping.

The instruction is the one Pennebaker's studies use. Write about something
that is genuinely bothering you, and write about what happened and how you
feel about it. Do not stop to fix a sentence. Spelling does not matter.
Nobody reads this. Nobody collects it. At the end you can keep it, bin it, or
burn it somewhere safer than campus.

If fifteen minutes on something real is too much today, write fifteen minutes
on something trivial instead. The mechanics are the point and you do not owe
anyone the subject matter. Passing entirely is also fine and needs no reason.

Then we talk about the *experience*, not the content. Was it a relief, a
grind, or did it make things louder? Did you write toward something or in
circles? Did fifteen minutes feel long?

## Then the uncomfortable part

Everyone recommends this. The evidence is much thinner than the
recommendations suggest.

Frattaroli (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*) ran the largest meta-analysis of
experimental disclosure. 146 randomised studies. The pooled effect was
positive and significant at **r = .075**, which is roughly **d = 0.15**. That
is small. Small enough that it sits below most of the other things on this
syllabus, including a ten-minute nap.

Two earlier meta-analyses had reported larger effects. They used fixed-effects
models, which assume every study is measuring one identical underlying thing.
Frattaroli used random effects across a bigger pool and the number came down.
That is a correction, not a scandal, but it is the sort of correction that
never reaches the blog posts.

It has not improved since. A 2023 meta-analysis in Asian populations found no
significant difference between expressive writing and control at first
follow-up, **g = 0.05**, or at second, **g = 0.04**.

What the meta-analysis does find is a pile of moderators, which is another way
of saying it depends. On who, on what about, on for how long, on how bad
things were to start with.

## Discussion

- You have just done the thing and you have just been told it barely
  registers. Which of those do you believe about your own fifteen minutes?
- This is the third technique this course has reported as smaller than its
  reputation, after the sugar and the phone break. What do those three have in
  common in how they got popular?
- A small average effect across 146 studies is compatible with it working
  well for some people and doing nothing for most. Is that a reason to try it
  or a reason to stop recommending it to everyone?
- Is there a version of this where the writing is not the active ingredient
  and the fifteen uninterrupted minutes are?
