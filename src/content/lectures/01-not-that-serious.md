## One sentence up front, unearned

The next four weeks are about the thing you already recognise: catching
yourself overthinking, out loud, in a room where everyone else is doing it
too. No framework first. You will have a stack of your own examples before
anybody offers you a theory about them.

But you get one sentence now, and it is deliberately unearned: **we think a
spiral repeats because the thinking never gets to finish.**

That is where the course gets its name, and it is the argument the whole
semester builds toward. It should not mean much to you yet — that is the
point. [The empty slot](/lectures/05-the-empty-slot/), in week 5, is where we
make the case properly and show you the research it rests on.

Take it as a promissory note. If it has not been paid by mid-semester, say so
on the forum.

## The first ten minutes

Every lecture and every crit in this course opens the same way: ten minutes
of guided attention, no phones, no talking, no outcome. We call it the detox,
which is a slightly silly name for sitting still, and the name is deliberate —
if it had a solemn name you would take it too seriously, which is the one
thing this course is against.

You will learn the format properly in week 3, when a meditation teacher runs
[Ten minutes, guided](/sessions/03-ten-minutes-guided/) and we stop
improvising. Until then it is: sit, breathe, notice, stop.

The evidence here is real but modest, and it is worth being precise about,
because "meditation fixes your brain" is a claim the internet makes and the
research does not. Jain and colleagues (2007) ran a randomised trial and found
mindfulness meditation reduced rumination more than relaxation training did —
so it is not just the sitting down. A 2022 meta-analysis of trials in depressed
patients found a small-to-moderate reduction in rumination overall. Meanwhile a
large controlled trial published in *Science Advances* in 2022 found no
structural brain changes from an eight-week mindfulness course compared to an
active control. So: a real effect on the thing we care about, no magic, and
anyone who tells you it rewires your brain is going further than the data.

## Why we are outside

Bratman and colleagues (2015, *PNAS*) took two groups on a ninety-minute walk
— one through a natural setting, one along a busy road. The nature group came
back reporting less rumination, and showed reduced activity in the subgenual
prefrontal cortex, a region associated with repetitive self-focused thought.
The urban group showed neither.

One study, one ninety-minute walk, one measure. It does not prove that holding
a class on a lawn makes anyone think better. But it is a good enough reason to
try it instead of a windowless room, and it gives us something to actually
watch for over the semester: does it work on you, or not?

You can suggest venues. The standing constraint is that everyone can get there
and everyone arrives on time — an outdoor class does not survive a slow start.

## The one technique for today

From Steven Hayes' work on acceptance and commitment therapy comes *cognitive
defusion*, and the simplest version of it is a sentence stem:

> I'm having the thought that I'm going to fail this.

Not "I'm going to fail this." The difference is six words and it is the whole
technique. It does not argue with the thought, disprove it, or replace it with
a nicer one — those all take the thought seriously enough to fight it. It just
puts a small gap between the thought and you, so the thought becomes something
you are having rather than something that is true.

We will use this stem all semester. It sounds ridiculous the first few times.
That is fine; so does the name detox.

## How this room works

Overthinking runs straight into things people have not told anyone. So, the
rules, stated once and then just true:

- **You can pass.** In any activity, at any point, without giving a reason.
  Passing is a normal move and nobody follows up.
- **Nothing you share is marked.** The assessments look at how you notice and
  reason, never at how much you were willing to say out loud.
- **This is a course, not therapy.** It is genuinely useful and it is not
  treatment. If something here opens a door you would rather have help with,
  ANU Counselling exists and is free.
- **What's said here stays here.** Including the funny things, which are
  usually the ones people most regret repeating.
