Lectures are paired with [crits](/sessions/) across the semester. All twelve
weeks are listed below so you can see the shape of the thing before you
commit to it. The back half is marked as provisional, because it is. Those
weeks get set by what the cohort votes to try, and a vote that could not
change anything would not be worth running. They carry the course's argument,
that a spiral repeats
because the thinking never gets the conditions under which it would finish,
along with the real research that argument rests on. They also mark,
repeatedly, the point where the popular version of that research runs ahead of
the evidence. Any claim on these pages with a name and a year attached is a
paper someone here has read. Anything without one is a hunch, and is labelled
as a hunch.

All of them are held **outdoors**, in a spot posted here by the Friday before.
**You pick the spot.** Suggest places on the Ed forum and vote. The only hard
constraints are that everyone can get there and everyone can arrive on time.
Because there is nowhere to wait around, these classes start on time and
cannot start late.

You also get a say in what they cover. If there is a topic you want treated
properly, propose it on the forum. Proposals are voted on, and the ones with
real support get worked up into later lectures. Several of the ones below
arrived that way.

Each lecture opens with the same ten minutes of guided attention that every
crit opens with. See
[Nothing here is that serious](/lectures/01-not-that-serious/) for what that is,
and why it has a slightly silly name.

## Why they are in this order

The first four weeks are about the symptom, not the mechanism. You spend them
catching yourself overthinking, the thing you already recognise, before anyone
hands you a framework for it. Week 5 is where the course finally makes its
argument, and where the title stops being a phrase and starts being a claim
you can test against your own examples.

That order has real research behind it, and one correction we had to make.
Starting concrete and moving toward the abstract reviews better than either
extreme (Fyfe, McNeil, Son and Goldstone, 2014). Schwartz and Bransford (1998)
found students who analysed cases *before* a lecture learned more than those
who were told first, which is the same rule the crits run on.

The correction came from the opposite literature. Luiten, Ames and Ackerson's
meta-analysis of 135 studies (1980) found a small but real benefit, around
d = 0.21 and larger in higher education, from giving learners a brief
organizing idea *up front*. Four weeks of activities with no stated claim is
exactly the failure that predicts. So week 1 states the argument in a single
sentence, calls it unearned, and then leaves it alone until week 5.

Two honest caveats, since this page is about being honest. The
concrete-to-abstract evidence comes mostly from maths and early science with
school-age learners, so applying it to a postgraduate course about
metacognition is an extrapolation rather than a finding. And one contested
*Science* paper found the reverse. Kaminski, Sloutsky and Heckler (2008)
report that undergraduates taught with generic symbols transferred *better*
than those taught with concrete examples, because concrete cases can bind what
you learn to their surface features. It drew substantial criticism, but it is
a real risk and worth knowing about a course built the other way round.

## Getting around a deck

Some lectures carry slides. They run in the browser with no on-screen
controls, so the keyboard drives them. Right arrow or space moves on, left
arrow goes back, **Escape** opens a grid of every slide, **G** jumps to a
numbered one, **F** is fullscreen and **S** opens the speaker notes. Press
**?** for the full list. Adding `?print-pdf` to a deck's address gives you a
printable version, one slide to a page.
