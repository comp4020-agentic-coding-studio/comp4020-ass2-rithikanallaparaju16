# Borrowed and renamed

Week 4. Provenance, and how to tell a real tradition from something a blogger
made up in 2014.

---

## Three weeks in, this course has handed you

- a sitting practice from South Asia
- a defusion technique from an American psychotherapy tradition
- an argument for going outside built on a Californian neuroimaging study

None of that is a problem. Not knowing it would be.

---

## What the ten minutes actually is

The standard story. Jon Kabat-Zinn took Theravāda Buddhist vipassanā, stripped
the religion out, called it Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, handed it to
medicine.

Kabat-Zinn (2011, *Contemporary Buddhism*) writes about the origins himself.
He does not hide the lineage.

---

## The tidy version is wrong in an interesting way

Husgafvel (2019, also *Contemporary Buddhism*) went through the history and
argues "MBSR equals vipassanā" is far too simple.

Kabat-Zinn was shaped substantially by Mahāyāna and Zen teachers and by
Tibetan Dzogchen. MBSR's philosophical footing diverges from Theravāda in ways
that matter.

## What "secular" hides

"We do a secular ten minutes" quietly implies the ten minutes was always
secular. It was not.

Something was removed to get it here. An ethical framework, a community, a
teacher, a purpose beyond feeling better.

Whether that was a reasonable trade or a significant loss is genuinely open.
You are allowed a view.

---

## Ikigai

You have met it as a four-circle diagram. What you love, what you are good at,
what the world needs, what you can be paid for. Sold as ancient Japanese
wisdom.

It was made by a British blogger, Marc Winn, in **2014**, by taking a 2011
Spanish purpose diagram by Andrés Zuzunaga and swapping one word.

---

## The real concept

Older, and real. Mieko Kamiya's *On the Meaning of Life* (1966) is a serious
treatment.

It has essentially nothing to do with being paid.

Ken Mogi, who writes on the concept, says plainly that the popular version is
wrong.

---

## Hoʻoponopono

Widely sold as a four-phrase mantra. *I'm sorry, please forgive me, thank you,
I love you.*

That version was popularised in the 2000s by Hew Len and Joe Vitale, propelled
by an unverifiable story about a psychiatric ward.

Traditional hoʻoponopono is a communal family process for repairing
relationships, led by a *kahuna lapaʻau*. Morrnah Simeona's 1980s adaptation
is the legitimate step in between.

---

## The repeatable pattern

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | A practice lives in a community, with a teacher and obligations |
| 2 | A Western populariser compresses it into a graphic or a mantra |
| 3 | It recirculates as ancient authenticity, stripped of the community |
| 4 | The locus moves from repairing something between people to optimising an individual |

# Step 4 is the one to notice

Both were about your relations with other people. The export turned each into
a private self-improvement tool.

That should be uncomfortable in a course which has so far treated overthinking
as a thing happening inside one head.

---

## A framework that disagrees with this course

Gee, Dudgeon, Schultz, Hart and Kelly (2014), in *Working Together*, set out
an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander social and emotional wellbeing
framework built on **seven domains of connection**.

Body. Mind and emotions. Family and kinship. Community. Culture. Country.
Spirituality and ancestors.

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## Mind and emotions is one of seven

Almost everything this course does sits in that single domain.

---

## Which is a different claim, not a nicer vocabulary

Catch your loop, notice your layer, build your empty slot. The other six
domains get treated as context at best.

A framework in which wellbeing is constituted by connection to family,
community, culture and Country is making a different claim about where a mind
is located.

Not resolved today. It may be part of why the techniques help less than
everyone hopes.

---

## Where the rest came from

- **The defusion stem** is Steven Hayes' ACT. American, about forty years old.
- **Going outside** rests on Bratman and colleagues (2015). The related
  *shinrin-yoku* was coined in 1982 by Tomohide Akiyama, then head of Japan's
  Forestry Agency. One review found 28 studies with methodological problems,
  and a recent meta-analysis rated every outcome "very low" certainty.
- **Slow breathing** is the weakest here. The six-breaths-a-minute figure
  rests on a narrative review. A pranayama meta-analysis found a small effect
  against passive control only, vanishing once high-risk-of-bias studies came
  out. Nobody has studied it on rumination.

---

## Argue with one of these

- The four-circle ikigai is more useful to more people than the real concept
  ever was. Does that matter? Argue both sides.
- If the community was part of the mechanism, exporting the technique without
  it is not simplification. It is removing an active ingredient. How would you
  test that?
- Take the seven domains seriously for ten minutes. Which crit would you
  redesign, and how?
- This course opens every class with a borrowed practice and has now spent a
  lecture on the ethics of borrowing. Is that sufficient, or is it just
  well-documented borrowing?
