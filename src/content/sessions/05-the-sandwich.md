## Why a sandwich is on the syllabus

Because it is the most precise instrument we have for the thing this course
is about.

The claim, argued properly in [week 5](/lectures/05-the-empty-slot/), is that
a spiral repeats because it never gets the conditions under which thinking
finishes — and that those conditions are surprisingly specific: an easy,
automatic task that occupies your hands and leaves your attention free.
Making a sandwich is that, exactly. So today we build the condition
deliberately, in a room full of people, and watch what happens.

Two outcomes are interesting and you should be ready for either. Your
thinking might settle something. Or the silence might hand your spiral an open
microphone, which is data of a different kind and worth just as much.

## Before the session

Bring ingredients for a sandwich you actually want to eat. Bread and one or
two fillings is plenty; this is not a cooking competition and there is no
prize for ambition. Tell us in advance about allergies and we will keep the
tables apart.

If you would rather not eat in front of people — for any reason, and you do
not have to say which — bring something to make and take away, or come and do
the discussion half. Say so beforehand or just turn up; both are fine.

## In the session

Forty-five minutes, no talking, phones away and face down. You make the
sandwich. You eat the sandwich. That is the entire activity.

No music. No podcast. Nobody explaining the exercise while you do it. If it
helps, notice what you are doing — the order you assemble things in, whether
you cut it, whether you already knew you cut it, which bite you liked, the
point at which you stopped tasting it and were just finishing it. But
noticing is not homework. Mostly: make it, eat it, be there.

Somewhere around minute six this stops being pleasant and starts being long.
That is the interesting part and it is not a sign you are doing it wrong.

## Then, the discussion

- What did you reach for that wasn't there?
- Where did your thinking actually go? Did anything resolve itself without
  you working on it — and if so, what had you been carrying?
- Did anyone's spiral get *louder* in the silence? That is the strongest
  objection to this whole crit and we should hear it.
- When did your attention leave, where did it go, and did it come back?
- Was the silence the hard part, or was it the not-multitasking?

## Why this, and how far the evidence goes

There is a real literature behind the eating half. Mindful-eating research
finds that training attention around food reduces automatic, unnoticed eating
patterns — a 2025 systematic review of the area reports genuine effects while
flagging small samples and mixed results, which is the honest summary rather
than the enthusiastic one.

Nobody, as far as we can tell, has run a controlled trial on making a sandwich
in silence. What this crit is doing is applying the mechanism from that
literature and from [the empty slot](/lectures/05-the-empty-slot/) — an
undemanding task with your attention left free — to the most ordinary thing we
could think of. That is an extrapolation, not a proven intervention, and you
should hold it that way.

The argument for the ordinary thing is that you already do it several times a
day. Whatever you find out here, you can go and check tomorrow.

## Afterwards

Nothing to submit. Optional, and worth doing: one meal this week with nothing
else running, and a single sentence about what turned up. That sentence is
audit material.

Keep whatever happened today in mind for [week 6](/lectures/06-the-blank-that-isnt/),
which looks at what usually occupies this slot instead — and at why filling it
with a phone feels like relief from a spiral while doing the opposite.
