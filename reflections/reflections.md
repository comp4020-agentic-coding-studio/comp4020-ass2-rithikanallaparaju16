## Why this course exists at all

Overthinking is universal and almost never taught. Everyone has re-read a
two-word reply too many times, or shelved a decision because thinking about it
more felt like progress. It costs people sleep, decisions and years, and no
degree anywhere treats it as a subject. It gets handled as a personality quirk
or as a clinical problem, with nothing in between — and the space in between is
where nearly everybody actually lives. That gap is the reason this course
exists.

## From naming it to doing something about it

The course started out as a way to *notice* overthinking. That is where most
writing on the subject stops, and it is the half that changes nothing on its
own — you end up better at watching yourself spiral.

So the course moved from diagnosis to pivot: what you actually do instead.
Catch the loop, then aim the same machinery somewhere useful, build the
conditions where thinking finishes, and know which of the things you reach for
under stress are worth reaching for. Every crit ends with something you can
do, not something you now understand. That shift is the most important
decision in the whole project.

## Why a lecture and a crit every single week

An earlier version of the schedule alternated — a lecture some weeks, a crit
others. I changed it, and the reason matters more than the timetable.

These are techniques, and techniques need practice at a rhythm rather than
exposure once a fortnight. Twice a week, every week, is what makes a habit
instead of a memory. The same reasoning gave every single class the same
ten-minute opening: the repetition *is* the teaching. It should be closer to
daily than weekly, which is why the daily practice is invited even though
nothing checks it.

The ambition behind that is deliberately larger than a semester. I want the
techniques to stick with students after the course ends, and to be simple
enough to explain to somebody else — the kind of thing that gets passed on
rather than filed. Most people already know the one thing about themselves
they would like to change; what they lack is a slightly easier way in. If a
student is still using one technique from this course in five years, or has
taught it to somebody, the course worked. Nothing on the assessment page
measures that, which is a limitation I am aware of and have not solved.

## Quality of life is the real subject

Not marks, and not a body of knowledge. Everything here is aimed at how the
day actually feels to live in.

Which is also why the course has to be humble about it. Nobody masters this —
not the students, not the staff, not the researchers whose papers are cited on
every lecture page. A course that implied otherwise would be selling
something. So the standing position, now written into the
[policies page](../src/pages/policies/index.mdx), is that this is not a thing
anyone finishes: you keep learning about it long after the semester, from
sources that have nothing to do with us. The log deliberately asks what
students found *elsewhere* that worked better than anything we taught, and
those answers change the syllabus.

## The assessment calendar is part of the argument

A course about not adding to your mental load has no business adding to it.

So the forum post lands in the mid-semester break, the capstone is due in week
9, the last three teaching weeks carry no deadline at all, and the log is due
24 hours after the *whole cohort's* last exam rather than after each student's
own. Nobody should ever be doing this course's work while revising for
something else. The weeks when everything else is due are weeks this course
spends being useful.

## Handing the syllabus to the room

The part I did not plan and now think is the best structural idea in the
course: students set some of it.

They vote on where each lecture happens. After the anonymous thread, every
technique the cohort reported is collected, anonymised and voted on, and the
winners get tried in class one per week — so the back half of the syllabus
grows out of what students actually found useful rather than what I guessed.
They can propose lecture topics, vote, and have the popular ones worked up
properly.

This is not decoration. It follows directly from the humility point above: if
nobody has mastered this, then the room collectively knows things the convenor
doesn't, and a syllabus that cannot absorb that is worse than one that can.

## Anonymity as a design tool

The first assignment is anonymous — to other students and to the teaching
staff — and students work in groups of six without being told who is in their
group unless they ask to be.

That was worth doing because the specific relief this course is trying to
produce is *the thing I thought was my private malfunction has eleven other
people in the thread*. Names get in the way of that. Marking runs off
participation data rather than content, so nobody grades a post against a
person, because nobody can.

## On AI, in a course about thinking

The course is strictly against AI writing any assessed work, and explicitly
encourages using it to research techniques.

The line is not about integrity theatre. An account of what you overthought,
written by a language model, is not a lighter version of the work — it is the
absence of it, and the only person it takes anything from is the student.
Research is different: going and finding what the internet recommends for
rumination is genuinely useful, and if a model surfaces a technique nobody
here has heard of, it goes on the list the room votes on. Research is
thinking. Outsourcing the noticing is not.

There is an obvious irony in building this site with an agent while telling
students not to. The distinction I would defend is the same one: the agent did
research I checked, and prose I directed and rewrote, on a fictional course —
it did not do my noticing for me, and PROCESS.md logs every prompt so anyone
can judge whether that line held.
