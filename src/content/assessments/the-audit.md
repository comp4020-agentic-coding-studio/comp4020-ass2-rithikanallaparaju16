## The brief

> Catch yourself doing it, and say honestly where you could have stopped.

The first six weeks gave you the tools: naming the layer past useful,
guessing-then-revealing the gap between the story and the outcome, an
undemanding slot built on purpose, and whatever you borrowed from someone else
in [How you stopped](/sessions/06-how-you-stopped/). This assessment asks you
to turn those tools on your own week, not on an exercise.

**Why it is due in week 6.** Because week 6 is quiet, and week 11 isn't. This
is the smaller of two assessments and it lands while you can still think
straight; see [Assessment](/assessments/) for the whole argument about when
things are due.

The log does not need drama. The moments that teach the most are often the
smallest — the ones where, in hindsight, you can see exactly where more
thinking stopped being useful and started being something else.

Nothing in the log has to be personal. A well-observed moment about a bus
timetable earns the same mark as one about a relationship, and picking the bus
timetable is not playing it safe — it is often the clearer specimen. Write
about what you are comfortable handing to a marker, and no further.

Two things that count as findings and are worth writing up if they happened
to you: the ten-minute practice doing nothing for you, and a technique the
room swore by failing on contact. Negative results are results.

## What you submit

A short document — the log plus the one unpacked moment — in whatever plain
format you like: markdown, a doc, even a photographed notebook page, as long
as it is legible. Say which moment is the unpacked one.
