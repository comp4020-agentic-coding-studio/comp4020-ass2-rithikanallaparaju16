Now the research behind them, and then the more useful question, which is how
big any of this is.

## The structural trick

Gollwitzer's distinction is between a goal intention, "I intend to reach
outcome X", and an implementation intention, "if situation Y happens, then I
will do X". The second one specifies when, where and how in advance.

The claim is that this moves the work. A goal intention needs you to decide
again, in the moment, while tired and already spiralling. An implementation
intention hands the deciding to the situation. You did the thinking in
advance, when you had some.

**The evidence.** Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006, *Advances in Experimental
Social Psychology*) meta-analysed 94 independent tests drawn from 63 reports
and found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, **d = .65**. That is
large for this field, and much larger than most things on this syllabus.

Three caveats, because a number that good deserves them.

First, the attribution gets muddled constantly. The figure is routinely
credited to Gollwitzer's 1999 paper, which introduced the idea. The
quantitative claim is from the 2006 meta-analysis.

Second, 2006 predates the publication-bias tools the field now runs as
standard. A meta-analysis from that era is not automatically wrong, but it
was assembled before anyone was routinely checking whether the small
null studies had been published at all.

Third, there is a newer synthesis. Sheeran, Listrom and Gollwitzer (2024)
aggregated 642 independent tests and, notably, declined to report one
headline number, framing the effect as conditional instead. When a literature
grows sevenfold and stops quoting a single figure, that is usually the
literature telling you something.

## Now the part that should change your capstone

Line up what this course has measured.

| Technique | Roughly |
| --- | --- |
| If-then plans | d = .65, from a 2006 meta-analysis with the caveats above |
| Worry postponement | d = 0.31 on duration, d = 0.19 on frequency |
| A single bout of exercise on state anxiety | about a quarter of a standard deviation |
| Expressive writing | r = .075, about d = 0.15 |
| Mandatory attendance policies | d = .21, from three studies |
| Sugar on mood | nothing, and worse alertness within the hour |

Most of what works, works a little. That is not a reason for despair, it is a
design constraint, and it is the single most useful thing to carry into the
capstone brief.

A few things follow from it.

**Small effects need cheap interventions.** If the lift is d = 0.2, an
activity that costs an hour and a lot of nerve is a bad trade. Ten minutes
and no disclosure is a good one.

**Averages hide people.** A small mean effect is equally consistent with a
technique that transforms a few people and does nothing for most. The
moderators in the writing literature are the visible edge of this. Designing
for "who is this for" beats designing for everyone.

**Watch what you are crediting.** The clearest lesson in the course is week
10's biscuit. You had a break and credited the sugar. Every intervention you
design has a plausible boring ingredient sitting next to the interesting one,
usually a stop, a limit, or another person.

## Where the honesty runs out

I cannot tell you the effect size of anything invented in this room, and
neither can anybody else. The course's own central claim, that a loop repeats
because it never gets the conditions to finish, has never been tested
directly. A capstone that designs something sensible, states plainly what it
is betting on, and says what would show it had failed, is doing the same
thing this course does. That is the standard, not proof.

## Discussion

- If most effects here are small, is the honest version of this course "these
  things help slightly", and would you have enrolled in that?
- The if-then number is the biggest on the syllabus and also the oldest and
  least re-examined. How much should age and scrutiny discount a finding?
- Pick any technique from the table. What is its boring ingredient?
- What would it take to convince you that something you invented worked, if
  you could not run a trial?
