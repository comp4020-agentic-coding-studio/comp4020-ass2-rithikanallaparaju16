## Before the crit

Notice, without changing anything yet, how fast you do three ordinary things.
Getting out of bed. Eating breakfast, if you eat it. Putting the shopping
away. Just clock the speed.

Bring something small to eat.

## The claim we are testing

Almost nobody decides to rush. You wake up already behind, so you eat standing
up, walk faster than you need to, unload the groceries as though someone is
timing you, and get through a day at a pace you never chose. A meal you were
supposed to sit down for happens in four minutes over a sink.

There is decent support for the general shape of that. Wood and Neal (2007)
describe habits as context-triggered responses marked by "efficiency, lack of
awareness, unintentionality", and Wood, Quinn and Kashy (2002) found roughly
43% of daily behaviour is habitual rather than deliberated. So much of what
you do is not chosen.

**The step from there to "and therefore your *speed* is a habit you never
chose" is mine, not theirs.** Nobody has studied pace as a habitual parameter
in the way this crit assumes. Hold it as a hypothesis you are about to test on
yourself.

One more finding, and it is a strange one. Robinson and Godbey's time-diary
work (*Time for Life*, 1997) found Americans had gained around an hour a day
of free time since the 1960s while reporting feeling *more* rushed. Feeling
hurried and being busy are not the same variable.

## In the crit

Forty minutes. Three rounds, no talking during them.

1. **Eat, at half speed.** Whatever you brought. Put it down between bites.
2. **Put something away, at half speed.** We will have a crate of odds and
   ends to unpack onto a table and repack. Deliberately unglamorous.
3. **One action of your own choosing**, done at half the speed you clocked
   this week.

Do not narrate it to yourself. That instruction matters and the reason is
below.

Eating in front of people is not neutral for everyone. Pass on the first
round and do a different action twice if you would rather, and you do not
have to say which one you swapped.

## What the evidence actually says

This is where the folk theory gets sorted into three piles, and one of the
piles will annoy you.

| The claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Eating slower changes something | **Well supported** |
| Attending to ordinary actions is worth practising | Mixed, with a real risk |
| **Walking slower is good for you** | **The evidence points the other way** |
| Slower groceries, showers, waking up | No evidence either way. My guess |

**Eating is the strong one.** Robinson and colleagues (2014, *American
Journal of Clinical Nutrition*) meta-analysed 22 experimental studies and
found a slower eating rate reduced energy intake, with a standardised effect
of about 0.45. That is a moderate effect, consistent across different ways of
slowing people down. Two honest caveats. Heterogeneity was high and the
samples were mostly healthy-weight young adults. And there was **no**
significant effect on how hungry people said they were, either at the end of
the meal or hours later. So it changes intake. It does not, on this evidence,
change appetite. Nobody in that meta-analysis measured *enjoyment*, which is
the thing the folk version promises.

**Walking is the one that contradicts us.** Michalak, Rohde and Troje (2015)
manipulated people's gait using biofeedback and found that a *reduced* walking
speed is part of the sad gait pattern, while walking in the "happy" style
produced more positive memory recall. Mood ratings themselves did not shift.
One small study, 39 people, and it is the only relevant experiment we could
find. But it points the opposite way to "walk slower and feel better." We are
keeping it on the syllabus as the counter-example rather than quietly
dropping it, because a course that only cited the studies agreeing with it
would be a worse course.

**And the rest is my hunch.** There is no research on unloading groceries
slowly, showering slowly, or waking up slowly. If you find some, the forum is
the place.

## The strongest objection to this whole crit

Take this seriously, because it comes from the same literature the course is
built on.

Mor and Winquist (2002, *Psychological Bulletin*) meta-analysed 226 effect
sizes and found that self-focused attention is associated with negative
affect. They also found that *rumination* produced stronger effects than
non-ruminative self-focus, with private self-focus most closely tied to
depression.

Now read the instructions above again. "Attend closely to what you are doing
with your own hands" is a request to increase private self-focused attention,
in a course whose entire premise is that too much attention on your own
interior is the problem. It is possible that this crit is a rumination
exercise with better branding.

The course is betting on a distinction here, and it is a bet. Attending to
*the action* is not the same as attending to *yourself doing the action*.
Noticing the weight of a jar is outward. Monitoring how well you are noticing
the weight of a jar is the trap. That is why you were told not to narrate.

Whether that distinction survives contact with a real room is one of the
things we find out today.

## Discussion

- Which of the three rounds was hardest, and was it the slowness or the
  silence?
- Did anyone tip from attending to the action into monitoring themselves? What
  did that feel like from the inside?
- Given Michalak, should the course tell you to walk *faster*?
- If pace really is a habit, Wood and Neal's work says the lever is the
  context, not the intention. What would you change about a morning rather
  than about your resolve?

## Afterwards

Pick one of the three and keep it at half speed for a week. Log it in
[the log](/assessments/the-log/). A week of "made no difference" is a result
worth having.
